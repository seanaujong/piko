Harbour Timing and Signal Practice

A harbour signal is a standing agreement between a shore station and every vessel that can see it. The agreement predates radio by centuries, and the shapes it uses were chosen to survive fog, rain and distance rather than to be beautiful. A cone hoisted point upward means one thing on every coast that has agreed to read it.

The timing rules matter more than the shapes. A signal raised at the wrong hour is not merely useless — it is actively misleading, because a master reading it will assume the hour was correct and infer the wrong condition. Stations therefore keep a log of every raising and lowering, and the log is the record consulted when accounts disagree.

Modern practice keeps the visual signals in place alongside the radio channels that replaced them. Redundancy of this kind is unfashionable and repeatedly justified: a vessel with a failed set still has eyes, and the shore station has no way of knowing which vessels can hear it. The cheapest signal is the one that needs no acknowledgement.

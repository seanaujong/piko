The Nature of Tides

A tide is the rise and fall of a sea level caused by the combined effects of gravitational forces exerted by the Moon and the Sun.[1] Tides originate in the oceans and progress towards the coastlines, where they appear as the regular rise and fall of the sea surface.

The U.S. National Ocean Service publishes tables of predicted heights. See Fig. 2 for a worked example covering a 3.5 metre range across one lunar cycle, which is roughly the span observed in the Bay of Fundy during spring tides.

When the Moon, the Earth and the Sun fall into alignment the resulting tide is called a spring tide, a name that has nothing to do with the season and everything to do with the water springing forth. The opposing arrangement, with the Sun at right angles to the Moon as seen from the Earth, produces the weaker neap tide.

Coastal communities have read these rhythms for as long as they have put boats in the water, long before anyone could account for them. The pattern was reliable well ahead of being explicable, which is the ordinary order of things.
